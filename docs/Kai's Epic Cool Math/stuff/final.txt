## 1.1 Background and Context

The continuously variable transmission (CVT) is a mechanical power transmission device capable of producing a continuously variable speed ratio between an input and output shaft, in contrast to the fixed discrete ratios of a conventional stepped gearbox. In the class of CVTs relevant to this work — dry rubber V-belt CVTs with mechanically actuated conical sheave pairs — the transmission ratio is not commanded by an external actuator or electronic controller. It is an emergent dynamic response: the balance of axial forces acting on the primary and secondary sheave pairs, arising from centrifugal flyweight mechanisms, torque-reactive helix and ramp assemblies, and return springs, determines the radial position at which the belt seats within each groove, and therefore the effective pitch radii and transmission ratio at every instant. This self-regulating character is simultaneously the CVT's greatest practical asset and the primary source of difficulty in modeling it rigorously — because no actuator explicitly prescribes the ratio, its trajectory at any instant is the output of a coupled mechanical system whose state depends on engine speed, load torque, belt clamping geometry, and the prior history of the shift position, all evolving together in time.

This class of CVT is found throughout small-displacement powersport and utility vehicles. It is the mandated transmission in the SAE Baja competition series, where a single-cylinder Briggs and Stratton 10 hp engine is paired with a CVT that must be designed, selected, and tuned entirely by student engineering teams from first principles. Beyond Baja, mechanically actuated rubber V-belt CVTs are standard equipment in snowmobiles, all-terrain vehicles (ATVs), utility task vehicles (UTVs), and small agricultural and industrial machinery. Despite this prevalence and the practical engineering importance of understanding their transient behavior, the quality of publicly available engineering models for these systems is strikingly uneven. The present work was motivated directly by that gap.

## 1.2 Motivation and Literature Review

### 1.2.1 Overview of the Modeling Landscape

A thorough review of CVT modeling literature reveals a consistent pattern: each body of work captures some aspects of the physics carefully while leaving equally important aspects absent, empiricized, or explicitly assumed away. A comprehensive survey of CVT dynamics and control research through 2009 is provided by **Srivastava and Haque** in their review article *"A Review of Belt and Chain Continuously Variable Transmissions: Dynamics and Control"*, published in *Mechanism and Machine Theory*, Vol. 44 (2009) [[B1]]. Their review systematically covers all major CVT types — rubber V-belt, metal pushing V-belt, and chain CVT — across static, quasi-static, and fully dynamic formulations, and surveys both the physical models and the control strategies built upon them. Crucially, it addresses rubber belt CVTs specifically, noting that the rubber belt CVT is mechanically and dynamically distinct from the metal pushing belt CVT in its actuation mechanism, belt compliance, and contact mechanics, and that the two should not be treated interchangeably in modeling. Their central conclusion — that there exists *"considerable disparity in the type of CVT models that have been used"* across the literature, and that fully dynamic, experimentally validated models remain scarce for several CVT classes — confirms that the field has advanced in fragments rather than toward a unified treatment. The present section builds on that foundation, updating the picture to reflect work published after the Srivastava and Haque review and focusing specifically on the shortcomings most relevant to a complete dynamic model for the mechanically actuated rubber V-belt CVT.

---

### 1.2.2 Metal Pushing V-Belt CVT Models

As documented in the Srivastava and Haque review, the most analytically complete CVT dynamic models in the literature concern the Van Doorne-type metal pushing V-belt CVT used in automotive applications, which is actuated hydraulically by electronically controlled oil pressure rather than by mechanical flyweight and spring mechanisms. Both the belt type and the actuation method are fundamentally different from the system studied here, and the physical mechanisms that govern their behavior — compressive segmented steel elements, hydraulically prescribed clamping force, and pulley flange bending deformation as the primary driver of belt re-seating — have no direct analog in a rubber tension belt under mechanical actuation. They are noted here because they represent the highest level of dynamic modeling rigor in the CVT literature and usefully illustrate what a complete treatment looks like, making the contrast with available rubber belt models all the more apparent.

**Carbone, Mangialardi, and Mantriota (2005)** derive a shifting dynamics model for the metal V-belt CVT in which the rate of ratio change is shown to scale with the logarithm of the clamping force ratio between pulleys, and two distinct shifting regimes are identified and validated experimentally: a creep mode in which ratio change is slow and governed by micro-slip redistribution, and a slip mode in which rapid ratio change is accompanied by macro-slip across the full contact arc. **Srivastava and Haque (2005, 2006)** extend the metal V-belt dynamic treatment by incorporating belt element inertia explicitly into the equations of motion — demonstrating that neglecting belt mass leads to underprediction of transient torque spikes during rapid ratio changes — and introduce a centrifugal correction to the normal force on belt elements at high speed. This centrifugal correction is the conceptual precursor to the centrifugal belt mass correction derived in Section 8 of the present work, though it is applied to discrete compressive metal elements rather than a continuous rubber belt. **Bonsen et al. (TU Eindhoven, ~2004)** characterize the slip-torque curve experimentally for the metal V-belt CVT and show that the relationship between slip ratio and transmitted torque follows an S-shaped curve, with transmitted torque reaching a maximum near the micro-slip to macro-slip transition. None of these models addresses mechanical actuation, rubber belt compliance, or vehicle coupling, and none is transferable to the rubber belt CVT without fundamental reformulation.

### 1.2.3 Rubber Belt Mechanical CVT Models

For the rubber V-belt CVT with mechanical actuation — the class directly relevant to this work — the literature is considerably thinner and modeling completeness drops sharply, a disparity explicitly noted in the Srivastava and Haque review [[B1]].

The most complete dynamic model for this class in the open literature is that of **Julio and Plante (~2011, Université de Sherbrooke)** [[C1]]. Their approach discretizes the belt into a finite number of nodal elements and applies Newton's second law to each node individually, computing local Coulomb friction forces from the local normal force at each contact point. This formulation has genuine strengths: the tension distribution around the wrap arc falls out naturally from the nodal equilibrium rather than being assumed to follow the classical capstan exponential; the wedging and self-locking behavior of the belt in the groove — wherein rapid clamping increases cause the belt to resist axial displacement — emerges automatically from the element-level geometry without special treatment; torque transfer is computed as the aggregate of tangential friction forces over all nodes in contact, physically detailed and implicitly self-limiting when nodes saturate at the Coulomb limit; and the model is validated against experimental data up to 150 Nm with good agreement. A centrifugal lever flyweight and torque-cam secondary mechanism are both included in the formulation.

Despite these strengths, Julio-Plante carries two significant and explicitly stated limitations that are of direct relevance to the present work. The first is that **the inertia of the movable sheaves is explicitly neglected** as a modeling assumption. Consequently, the sheave axial position is resolved at each timestep by satisfying an instantaneous force balance rather than by integrating an equation of motion with physical mass, damping, and net force. This means the model does not capture the shift lag physically caused by sheave inertia — the finite delay between when a net axial force first acts on the sheave and when the sheave has actually displaced — which is a physically significant effect during any rapid transient. The second, and directly consequent, limitation is that **the model is unable to handle clutching conditions**. The authors explicitly state that *"it has been impossible to obtain stabilized model predictions"* during the transition between the clutching mode and the shifting mode, and they propose extension to clutching conditions as a named future work item. This instability is a direct consequence of the nodal force-balance approach encountering the highly nonlinear and rapidly evolving contact geometry of belt engagement from rest without a damped inertial axial equation of motion to regularize the dynamics through it. This is not a minor omission: mechanically actuated rubber belt CVTs use the belt clamping mechanism as the start-up clutch directly, with no separate external clutching device, meaning the clutching regime governs every vehicle launch from rest and is a first-class part of the operating envelope. Beyond these two gaps, Julio-Plante does not include the centrifugal belt mass correction to effective clamping force, does not couple the model to vehicle longitudinal dynamics, and produces a numerically assembled simulation rather than a closed analytical system that is tractable for sensitivity analysis, linearization, or analytical tuning insight.

**SAE Paper 2011-32-0518**, *"Development of CVT Shift Dynamic Simulation Model with Elastic Rubber V-Belt,"* develops a simulation model specifically for the shift dynamics of a small-vehicle rubber V-belt CVT, treats the belt as elastic rather than rigid, and derives a differential equation governing the shift motion. It represents, to the extent the abstract and secondary sources reveal, one of the more direct prior attempts at a shift dynamics ODE for this CVT class. The full paper is paywalled and the complete derivation is not publicly accessible; based on available information, the actuation mechanism appears empirically parameterized rather than derived from first principles, explicit slip-state switching is not described, the centrifugal belt correction is not included, and vehicle dynamics coupling is absent.

Several further studies address subsets of the rubber belt CVT physics in isolation. A study published in the *Annals of the Faculty of Engineering Hunedoara* (2013) derives a static relationship between clamping force and maximum transmittable torque for a rubber V-belt CVT and validates it experimentally — a useful torque capacity characterization, but a static analysis only, with no equations of motion, no actuation model, and no transient behavior. A MATLAB/Simulink dynamic model presented in a Koreascience publication (2011) implements belt tension dynamics and examines performance characteristics, but uses empirical lookup curves for flyweight actuation and the secondary spring, does not include an explicit slip model, and omits the centrifugal belt correction. None of these approaches constitutes a complete dynamic treatment.

### 1.2.4 Quasi-Static and Static Baja-Specific Models

In the SAE Baja context specifically, the dominant modeling paradigm in the literature is the quasi-static or static force balance. These models solve the algebraic equilibrium condition — the set of operating points at which net axial force on the sheave is zero — at each operating condition independently, treating the shift position as an instantaneous function of the inputs rather than as a dynamic state with its own equation of motion. They can characterize where the CVT wants to be at a given RPM and load, but carry no information about how fast it gets there, what happens during a transient, or whether the belt slips in the process.

**Skinner (2020, West Virginia University)** [[D1]] is the most widely referenced example. It develops a quasi-static force balance for both the flyweight-actuated primary and the ramp-actuated secondary, characterizes engagement speed, shift-out speed, and static operating RPM, and presents a sensitivity analysis showing how flyweight mass, spring preload, spring rate, and ramp angle move these operating points. It is a practically useful tuning tool and the static force balance structure it establishes is, in many respects, the correct physical starting point for the derivation in the present work. Its fundamental limitation is that it is entirely static: the shift position has no equation of motion, no inertia, no damping, and no time dependence. Belt slip is absent throughout — the belt is treated as an ideal lossless torque coupler at all operating conditions. The centrifugal contribution of rotating belt mass to effective clamping force is not included. The secondary helix geometry is simplified to a constant ramp angle. No vehicle longitudinal dynamics are coupled. As a result, it cannot simulate a ratio trajectory over time, cannot predict slip onset under a given transient load, cannot compute actual output torque during shifting, and cannot simulate engagement dynamics from rest. **Anderson (2017)** and the **2025 ASEE paper from Marshall University** represent similar quasi-static implementations with the same structural limitations.

### 1.2.5 The Persistent Gap

The survey above, summarized in Table 1.1, reveals a clear and consistent pattern. For the mechanically actuated dry rubber V-belt CVT — the system class addressed in this work — no existing publicly available model simultaneously achieves all of the following:

1. An **explicit equation of motion for the primary sheave axial shift** with physical inertia and damping, integrable forward in time through both the shifting and clutching regimes
2. **First-principles derivation of the flyweight actuation force** from rotating-frame Newtonian mechanics, with full position and velocity dependence
3. **First-principles derivation of the secondary axial force** from position-varying helix geometry and torque-reactive coupling
4. A **centrifugal belt mass correction** to effective sheave clamping force, accounting for the tendency of the rotating belt to radially unseat from the conical groove at speed
5. An **explicit analytical slip-state switching criterion** that cleanly separates the no-slip torque transmission regime from the traction-limited slip regime, governed by a closed-form torque capacity expression
6. **Full coupling to vehicle longitudinal dynamics** within the same state-space framework

The metal V-belt models of Carbone, Srivastava and Haque, and Bonsen et al. are the most dynamically rigorous treatments in the CVT literature broadly, but they address an incompatible system class under hydraulic actuation and are not transferable here. Julio-Plante is the closest prior work for the rubber belt mechanical CVT and is a genuine contribution, but explicitly neglects movable sheave inertia, cannot produce stable predictions during the clutching regime by the authors' own account, omits the centrifugal belt correction, and does not couple to vehicle dynamics. The quasi-static Baja models of Skinner and others provide correct physical intuition and static force balance structure but cannot simulate transient behavior by construction. As Srivastava and Haque observe in their review, considerable disparity persists across CVT model types and fully dynamic unified treatments remain scarce [[B1]]. This document addresses that gap.

## 1.3 Objectives and Scope

This document has two parallel and mutually reinforcing objectives.

**The first is technical:** to derive a complete, closed, coupled system of nonlinear ordinary differential equations governing the CVT from engine input shaft to vehicle wheel, suitable for direct numerical integration in time. The model is intended as the CVT subsystem of a broader vehicle dynamics simulation framework — capable of predicting, as continuous time-domain trajectories, the transmission ratio, belt slip state, torque delivered to the output shaft, and the resulting vehicle longitudinal acceleration and speed from any prescribed initial condition and engine input.

**The second is pedagogical:** to make every step of the derivation explicit, traceable, and physically interpretable, such that a reader who works through the document develops genuine understanding of the mechanisms governing CVT behavior — not simply a set of equations to implement. This objective is directly motivated by the practical reality of CVT design and tuning: engineers and teams who understand *why* the ratio shifts at a particular speed, *why* slip onset depends on clamping force geometry, and *why* the helix angle of the secondary torque cam couples output torque to clamping force are far more effective at diagnosing and tuning CVT behavior than those relying on empirical trial and error. Every result in this document is derived from governing principles; nothing is asserted without justification.

The immediate application context is the SAE Baja CVT, parameterized for a flyweight-actuated primary clutch and a helix-and-spring secondary, as used on a student competition vehicle. The derivation framework is intentionally generic and applies to any dry rubber V-belt CVT with flyweight-actuated primary and ramp- or helix-actuated secondary — it can be re-parameterized for other systems by substituting appropriate geometric, inertial, and spring data.

**What this model covers:**
- Primary sheave axial shift dynamics: explicit equation of motion with inertia, damping, centrifugal flyweight actuation, spring loading, and belt axial reaction force, valid through both the shifting and clutching regimes
- Secondary sheave axial force balance: position-varying helix geometry, torque-reactive cam coupling, spring preload, and belt axial reaction
- Belt kinematic geometry: wrap angles, pitch radii, and transmission ratio as functions of primary axial shift position
- Belt traction model: generalized capstan equation with centrifugal belt mass correction to effective clamping force
- Explicit slip-state logic: closed-form torque capacity criterion with discrete switching between no-slip torque transmission and traction-limited sliding regimes
- Primary and secondary rotational equations of motion: engine torque input, belt coupling torque, and drivetrain load
- Vehicle longitudinal dynamics: one-dimensional force balance including rolling resistance, aerodynamic drag, and gravitational grade loading

**What this model does not cover:**
- Thermal effects or temperature-dependent material properties
- Belt wear, surface degradation, or long-term compliance evolution
- Detailed tribological contact mechanics including rubber viscoelastic hysteresis, Stribeck friction behavior, or speed-dependent friction coefficients
- Drivetrain torsional compliance or multi-body vehicle dynamics beyond one-dimensional longitudinal motion

## 1.4 Contribution Statement

The specific contributions of this work are as follows:

1. **A unified, closed, analytically tractable state-space dynamic model** for the mechanically-actuated dry rubber V-belt CVT, integrating actuation dynamics, belt traction mechanics, slip-state logic, and vehicle longitudinal response in a single coupled system integrable from any initial condition.

2. **First-principles derivation of the primary sheave axial shift equation of motion**, explicitly including sheave inertia and damping and deriving the centrifugal flyweight actuation force from rotating-frame Newtonian mechanics — rather than treating it as an empirical force input — valid continuously through both the shifting and clutching operating regimes.

3. **First-principles derivation of the secondary axial force model**, including the position-varying helix geometry and the coupling between output torque and secondary clamping force that constitutes the torque-reactive character of the secondary mechanism.

4. **Derivation of the centrifugal belt mass correction** to effective sheave clamping force — the reduction in normal contact force at the sheave groove caused by the tendency of the rotating belt to radially unseat at speed — a physically important correction at competition operating speeds absent from all Baja CVT models identified in the literature.

5. **An explicit, analytically derived slip-state switching criterion**, cleanly separating the no-slip torque transmission regime from the traction-limited slip regime, with the transition governed at each instant by a closed-form torque capacity expression derived from the generalized capstan equation.

6. **Full coupling of the CVT dynamic model to vehicle longitudinal dynamics** within a single state-space system, enabling end-to-end simulation from engine throttle input to vehicle acceleration and speed.

7. **A self-contained, pedagogically complete derivation** structured to build physical intuition alongside mathematical rigor, intended to support both simulation implementation and practical understanding of CVT design and tuning.
