## 1.1 Background and Context

The continuously variable transmission (CVT) is a mechanical power transmission device capable of producing a continuously variable speed ratio between an input and output shaft, in contrast to the fixed discrete ratios of a conventional stepped gearbox. In the class of CVTs relevant to this work — dry rubber V-belt CVTs with mechanically actuated conical sheave pairs — the transmission ratio is not commanded by an external actuator or electronic controller. Instead, it is an emergent dynamic response: the equilibrium of axial forces acting on the primary and secondary sheave pairs, arising from centrifugal flyweight mechanisms, torque-reactive helix and ramp assemblies, and return springs, determines the radial position at which the belt seats within each sheave groove, and therefore the effective pitch radii and transmission ratio at every instant.

This self-regulating character is simultaneously the CVT's greatest practical asset and the primary source of difficulty in modeling it rigorously. Because no actuator explicitly commands the ratio, the ratio trajectory at any instant is the output of a coupled mechanical system whose state depends on engine speed, load torque, belt clamping geometry, and the prior history of the shift position — all evolving together in time.

This class of CVT is found throughout small-displacement powersport and utility vehicles. It is the mandated transmission in the SAE Baja competition series, where a single-cylinder Briggs and Stratton 10 hp engine is paired with a CVT that must be designed, selected, and tuned entirely by student engineering teams from first principles. Beyond Baja, mechanically actuated rubber V-belt CVTs are standard equipment in snowmobiles, all-terrain vehicles (ATVs), utility task vehicles (UTVs), and small agricultural and industrial machinery. Despite their prevalence and the practical engineering importance of understanding their behavior, the quality of publicly available engineering models for these systems is strikingly uneven. The present work was motivated directly by this gap.

## 1.2 Motivation and Literature Review

### 1.2.1 The Modeling Landscape and Its Fragmentation

A thorough review of the CVT modeling literature reveals a consistent pattern: each body of work captures some aspects of CVT physics with care while leaving equally important aspects either absent, empiricized, or explicitly assumed away. No single openly available model for the mechanically actuated dry rubber V-belt CVT appears to simultaneously derive — in closed analytical form and from first principles — the actuation dynamics, belt traction mechanics, slip-state transition logic, and vehicle coupling as a unified, integrable dynamic system. This section surveys the relevant literature in detail, identifies the specific physical elements each contribution addresses, and traces precisely where and why each falls short of a complete treatment.

It is useful to organize this survey into four groups: foundational belt mechanics, metal pushing V-belt CVT models (which represent the most analytically complete CVT dynamic work in the literature), rubber belt mechanical CVT models (most directly relevant to this work), and small-vehicle or Baja-specific models.

---

### 1.2.2 Foundational Belt Mechanics

The earliest rigorous treatment of slip in rubber V-belt systems is due to **Gerbert (1996)**, whose paper *"Belt Slip — A Unified Approach"* in the ASME Journal of Mechanical Design remains the foundational reference for belt-pulley contact zone mechanics. Gerbert decomposes belt deformation into four physically distinct contributions — elastic creep along the free span, radial compliance of the belt cross-section as it seats into the groove, shear deflection both radially and laterally, and flexural rigidity effects at the entry and exit of the contact arc — and shows that classical Euler capstan theory, which treats the belt as inextensible and the contact arc as uniformly loaded, substantially underpredicts the actual slip at small pulley diameters because it neglects compliance-driven mechanisms. Gerbert's work is essential background for any belt traction model and is the basis on which the generalized capstan formulation used in Section 10 of this document rests. It is, however, a steady-state contact zone analysis. Time does not appear in the model. There is no equation of motion, no shifting mechanism, no axial force model, and no actuation. It characterizes the belt-pulley interface at a fixed operating point; it does not simulate a CVT.

**Micklem, Clifton, and Hotter (1994)** in the *Proceedings of the Institution of Mechanical Engineers, Part C*, develop equilibrium equations for belt element forces around the wrap arc of a metal V-belt, introducing the concept of distinct compression and tension zones on the driven and drive pulleys respectively — a refinement on the uniform-arc assumption of capstan theory. Like Gerbert, this is a static equilibrium analysis: it produces force distributions at a given operating point but no time-domain dynamics, no actuation model, and no shift equation.

### 1.2.3 Metal Pushing V-Belt CVT Models

The most analytically complete CVT dynamic models in the literature concern the Van Doorne-type metal pushing V-belt CVT used in automotive applications, which is hydraulically actuated and uses compressive segmented steel elements rather than a rubber tension belt. While the belt type and actuation mechanism are both fundamentally different from the mechanically actuated rubber belt CVT of interest here, this body of work is important to survey because it represents the state of the art in CVT dynamic modeling rigor, and its limitations relative to the present problem class are instructive.

**Carbone, Mangialardi, and Mantriota (2005)**, in the ASME Journal of Mechanical Design, develop a detailed analytical model of the shifting dynamics for the metal V-belt CVT, explicitly accounting for pulley flange bending deformation as a key physical mechanism driving belt re-seating during ratio change. Their central result — that the shift rate <latex>\dot{s}</latex> is proportional to primary angular velocity and scales with the logarithm of the clamping force ratio between pulleys — represents a genuine contribution to understanding how ratio change emerges dynamically from the coupled belt-pulley-deformation system. They identify and distinguish two shifting regimes: a creep mode in which ratio change is slow and driven by micro-slip redistribution, and a slip mode in which rapid ratio change is accompanied by macro-slip across the contact arc. This creep/slip distinction is the metal belt analog of the no-slip vs. slipping regime switching that appears in the present work. The model is validated experimentally. However, the physical mechanisms are specific to hydraulic actuation and the metal belt: pulley deformation is the driver of belt re-seating, clamping force is controlled externally by hydraulic pressure, and the belt's compressive element dynamics have no analog in a rubber tension belt. The model produces no flyweight dynamics, no helix or ramp geometry, no centrifugal belt mass correction, and no vehicle coupling.

**Srivastava and Haque (2006)**, in the ASME Journal of Computational and Nonlinear Dynamics, extend this line of work by incorporating belt inertia — specifically the rotational kinetic energy of the belt element array — explicitly into the equations of motion for the first time. They show that neglecting belt mass leads to underprediction of transient torque spikes during rapid ratio change. Their companion paper (2005) addresses high-speed operation specifically and introduces a centrifugal force correction to the normal force acting on belt elements at speed — the conceptual ancestor of the centrifugal belt mass correction derived in Section 8 of the present work, though applied to metal elements rather than a continuous rubber belt. Both papers are confined to hydraulic actuation and the metal belt; neither addresses mechanical actuation, rubber belt compliance, nor vehicle coupling.

**Bonsen et al. (TU Eindhoven, ~2003–2005)** produce a series of papers on CVT slip identification and control. Their key experimental contribution is the characterization of the slip-torque curve: the S-shaped relationship between belt slip ratio and transmitted torque that shows transmitted torque rising steeply with small amounts of slip, reaching a maximum near the macro-slip transition, and then degrading with full sliding. They also introduce the practically important finding that operating a CVT at a controlled small slip setpoint improves variator efficiency relative to fully clamped operation. This is a genuine empirical contribution to the understanding of the micro-slip to macro-slip transition, but the work is entirely in the context of hydraulically actuated metal belt CVTs, and the slip characterization is obtained from experiments rather than derived analytically from a first-principles traction model.

### 1.2.4 Rubber Belt Mechanical CVT Models

Moving to the rubber V-belt CVT with mechanical actuation — the class directly relevant to this work — the literature thins considerably and the modeling completeness drops sharply.

**Julio and Plante (~2011, Université de Sherbrooke)** present what is, to the authors' knowledge, the most complete dynamic model for a mechanically-actuated rubber V-belt CVT in the open literature prior to the present work. Their approach discretizes the belt into a finite number of nodal elements and applies Newton's second law to each node individually, computing local Coulomb friction forces at each contact point from the local normal force. This nodal formulation has several genuine strengths: the tension distribution around the wrap arc is not assumed to follow the classical capstan exponential but falls out naturally from the nodal equilibrium, the wedging and self-locking behavior of the belt in the groove (wherein rapid clamping increases cause the belt to resist axial displacement) emerges automatically from the node-level geometry rather than requiring separate modeling, and torque transfer is computed as the aggregate of tangential friction forces over all nodes in contact with each pulley — physically detailed and implicitly self-limiting when nodes saturate at their Coulomb limit. The model is validated experimentally against load cases up to 150 Nm with good agreement and does include both a centrifugal lever (flyweight) actuation mechanism and a torque-cam secondary. It is, in this sense, a dynamic model of a mechanically actuated rubber belt CVT, and this must be acknowledged clearly.

However, the Julio-Plante model is not a closed-form analytical dynamic system, and this distinction is consequential. The flyweight and torque-cam actuation forces are modeled as prescribed or empirically parameterized inputs to the sheave, not derived from first principles within the same framework. More critically, the sheave axial position in their formulation appears to adjust each timestep by satisfying an axial force balance rather than by integrating an explicit equation of motion <latex>m_{\text{eff}}\ddot{s} = F_{\text{net}}</latex> with physical inertia and damping. This means the shift lag that physically results from sheave inertia — the delay between when a net axial force first acts and when the sheave has actually displaced — is not captured. For slow, steady-state operating condition changes this may be negligible; for the rapid transients characteristic of Baja competition driving (throttle blips, bump-induced load spikes, engagement from rest), sheave inertia is physically significant and its omission will cause the model to overpredict shift speed. Additionally, the nodal belt formulation, while physically detailed at the contact zone level, is not analytically tractable: there is no closed-form expression for torque capacity as a function of clamping force and geometry, no analytical sensitivity of shift dynamics to design parameters, and no clean Jacobian for linearization or control design. The model also does not include the centrifugal belt mass correction to effective clamping force — the reduction in normal force at the sheave contact caused by the centrifugal tendency of the rotating belt to unseat from the groove. Finally, the model is not coupled to vehicle longitudinal dynamics. Taken together, Julio-Plante represents the closest prior work to the present model and is a genuine contribution to rubber belt CVT dynamics, but it is a numerically assembled simulation framework rather than a closed, first-principles, analytically tractable dynamic system.

**SAE Paper 2011-32-0518**, *"Development of CVT Shift Dynamic Simulation Model with Elastic Rubber V-Belt,"* develops a simulation model specifically for shift dynamics in small-vehicle rubber V-belt CVTs, treats the belt as elastic rather than rigid, and derives a differential equation governing the shift motion. It is, to the extent the abstract and secondary sources reveal, the most direct prior attempt at a shift dynamics ODE for this CVT class. The full paper is paywalled and the complete derivation is not publicly accessible; however, based on available information, the actuation mechanism appears to be empirically parameterized rather than derived from first principles, explicit slip-state switching is not modeled, the centrifugal belt correction is not included, and no vehicle dynamics coupling is described.

**A study published in the Annals of the Faculty of Engineering Hunedoara (2013)** analyzes belt slip behavior and torque capacity experimentally and analytically for a rubber V-belt CVT. It derives a static relationship between clamping force magnitude and maximum transmittable torque, and validates it experimentally — a useful characterization of the torque capacity boundary. It is, however, entirely a static analysis: there are no equations of motion, no shift dynamics, no actuation model, and no transient behavior.

**Koreascience (~2011)** presents a MATLAB/Simulink dynamic model of a rubber belt CVT system and examines both static and dynamic performance characteristics, including belt tension dynamics. The flyweight actuation and secondary spring forces are modeled through empirical lookup curves rather than geometric derivations, no explicit slip model is implemented, centrifugal belt mass effects are absent, and the helix geometry is not derived.

### 1.2.5 Baja SAE and Small-Vehicle Specific Models

**Skinner (2020)**, *"Modeling and Tuning of CVT Systems for SAE Baja Vehicles,"* West Virginia University, is the most widely cited prior work in the SAE Baja context and represents a well-structured, practically oriented contribution. Skinner develops a quasi-static force balance model for the flyweight-actuated primary and ramp-actuated secondary, derives force balance equations for both subsystems, and uses them to characterize three key operating conditions: engagement speed, shift-out speed, and static operating RPM. The tuning sensitivity analysis — how changing flyweight mass, spring preload, spring rate, and ramp angle moves these three operating points — is clear and useful. The MATLAB implementation provides a practical tuning tool for Baja teams. The physical intuition and the static force balance structure that Skinner establishes are, in many ways, the correct starting point for the derivation in the present work.

What Skinner's model fundamentally cannot do, however, follows directly from its static nature. The sheave displacement has no equation of motion: there is no inertia, no damping, and no explicit time dependence in the shift position. The model solves the equilibrium manifold — the set of operating points where net axial force is zero — and identifies particular points on that manifold as the engagement and shift-out conditions. It tells you where the CVT wants to be at each RPM, not how fast it gets there or what happens dynamically along the way. Belt slip is entirely absent; the belt is an ideal, lossless torque coupler at all operating conditions. The centrifugal contribution of rotating belt mass to the effective clamping force is not included. The secondary helix geometry is simplified to a constant ramp angle. No vehicle longitudinal dynamics are coupled. As a result, Skinner's model cannot simulate a ratio trajectory over time, cannot predict whether a slip event occurs under a given load, cannot compute the actual output torque during a transient shift, and cannot predict engagement dynamics from rest.

**Messick (2018)**, *"An Experimentally-Validated V-Belt Model for Axial Force and Efficiency in a CVT,"* Virginia Tech, focuses specifically on predicting axial forces and mechanical efficiency in a rubber V-belt CVT for SAE Baja, using a custom instrumented dynamometer for validation. It is an axial force and efficiency characterization: no shift dynamics ODE, no flyweight or secondary actuation mechanism model, no slip or torque capacity modeling, and no vehicle coupling.

**Anderson (2017)**, a standalone technical analysis from Stanford, presents a quasi-static flyweight CVT analysis with a MATLAB tuning implementation. It derives belt kinematic geometry and flyweight centrifugal force clearly and is a well-organized reference for the static force balance structure, but is static only: no equation of motion, no slip model, no centrifugal belt correction, constant helix angle assumption, no vehicle dynamics.

**A 2025 ASEE paper from Marshall University** presents a predictive MATLAB model for mechanical CVT performance validated on a dynamometer. It characterizes flyweight dynamics and spring pretension effects to predict shift curves, but is empirically parameterized throughout, contains no slip model, no belt dynamics derivation, no helix geometry, and no vehicle coupling.

### 1.2.6 The Persistent Gap

The preceding survey, summarized in Table 1.1, reveals a consistent and striking pattern. For the specific class of CVT addressed in this work — the mechanically actuated dry rubber V-belt CVT — no existing publicly available model simultaneously achieves all of the following:

1. An explicit, physically derived **equation of motion for the primary sheave axial shift** with inertia and damping — not a quasi-static force balance, but an ODE integrable forward in time
2. **First-principles derivation of the flyweight actuation force** from rotating-frame mechanics, with position- and velocity-dependent centrifugal and Coriolis contributions
3. **First-principles derivation of the secondary axial force** from the position-varying helix geometry and torque-reactive cam mechanics
4. A **centrifugal belt mass correction** to the effective sheave clamping force, accounting for the tendency of the rotating belt to radially unseat from the groove at speed
5. An **explicit slip-state switching criterion** — an analytical torque capacity expression that triggers a discrete transition between no-slip torque coupling and traction-limited slip at each timestep, with both regimes governed by separate physics
6. **Full coupling to vehicle longitudinal dynamics** in the same state-space framework

The metal V-belt models of Carbone, Srivastava, and the TU Eindhoven group are the most dynamically complete treatments in the CVT literature broadly, but address an incompatible belt type under hydraulic actuation. The Julio-Plante model is the closest prior work for the rubber belt mechanical CVT and includes genuine dynamics, but uses a numerically assembled nodal belt formulation with empirically parameterized actuation, no sheave inertia ODE, no centrifugal belt correction, and no vehicle coupling. Skinner provides the correct physical intuition and static structure but is entirely equilibrium-based. No model reviewed achieves all six properties simultaneously.

This document addresses that gap.

## 1.3 Objectives and Scope

This document has two parallel and mutually reinforcing objectives.

**The first is technical:** to derive a complete, closed, coupled system of nonlinear ordinary differential equations governing the CVT from engine input shaft to vehicle wheel, suitable for direct numerical integration in time. The model is intended as the CVT subsystem of a broader vehicle dynamics simulation framework — capable of predicting, as continuous time-domain trajectories, the transmission ratio, belt slip state, torque delivered to the output shaft, and the resulting vehicle longitudinal acceleration and speed from any prescribed initial condition and engine input.

**The second is pedagogical:** to make every step of the derivation explicit, traceable, and physically interpretable, such that a reader who works through the document develops genuine understanding of the mechanisms governing CVT behavior — not simply a set of equations to implement. This objective is directly motivated by the practical reality of CVT design and tuning: engineers and teams who understand *why* the ratio shifts at a particular speed, *why* slip onset depends on clamping force geometry, and *why* the helix angle of the secondary torque cam couples output torque to clamping force are far more effective at diagnosing and tuning CVT behavior than those who rely on empirical trial and error. Every result in this document is derived from governing principles; nothing is asserted without justification.

The immediate application context is the SAE Baja CVT: the system is parameterized for a flyweight-actuated primary clutch and a helix-and-spring secondary, as used on a student competition vehicle. The derivation framework is, however, intentionally generic. The model structure applies to any dry rubber V-belt CVT with flyweight-actuated primary and ramp- or helix-actuated secondary, and can be re-parameterized for other systems by substituting appropriate geometric, inertial, and spring data.

**What this model covers:**
- Primary sheave axial shift dynamics: equation of motion with inertia, damping, centrifugal flyweight actuation, spring loading, and belt axial reaction force
- Secondary sheave axial force balance: position-varying helix geometry, torque-reactive cam coupling, spring preload, and belt axial reaction
- Belt kinematic geometry: wrap angles, pitch radii, and ratio as functions of axial shift position
- Belt traction model: generalized capstan equation with centrifugal belt mass correction
- Explicit slip-state logic: analytical torque capacity criterion with discrete switching between no-slip and traction-limited regimes
- Primary and secondary rotational equations of motion: engine torque input, belt coupling torque, drivetrain load
- Vehicle longitudinal dynamics: one-dimensional force balance with rolling resistance, aerodynamic drag, and gravitational grade loading

**What this model does not cover:**
- Thermal effects or temperature-dependent material properties
- Belt wear, surface degradation, or long-term compliance evolution
- Detailed tribological contact mechanics (rubber hysteresis, Stribeck friction curve, speed-dependent friction coefficient)
- Drivetrain torsional compliance or multi-body vehicle dynamics beyond one-dimensional longitudinal motion

## 1.4 Contribution Statement

The specific contributions of this work are as follows:

1. **A unified, closed, analytically tractable state-space dynamic model** for the mechanically-actuated dry rubber V-belt CVT, governing the full coupled evolution of sheave shift position, primary and secondary angular velocities, and vehicle speed from any initial condition.

2. **First-principles derivation of the primary axial shift equation of motion**, explicitly including sheave inertia and damping, and deriving the flyweight centrifugal actuation force from rotating-frame Newtonian mechanics rather than treating it as an empirical input.

3. **First-principles derivation of the secondary axial force model**, including the position-varying helix geometry and the coupling between output torque and secondary clamping force that constitutes the torque-reactive character of the secondary.

4. **Derivation of the centrifugal belt mass correction** to effective sheave clamping force — the reduction in normal contact force caused by the tendency of the rotating belt to radially unseat from the conical groove at speed — a physically important correction at competition operating speeds that is absent from all Baja CVT models identified in the literature.

5. **An explicit, analytically derived slip-state switching criterion**, cleanly separating the no-slip torque transmission regime from the traction-limited slip regime, with the transition governed by a closed-form torque capacity expression derived from the generalized capstan equation.

6. **Full coupling of the CVT dynamic model to vehicle longitudinal dynamics** within a single state-space system, enabling end-to-end simulation from engine speed and throttle input to vehicle acceleration and speed.

7. **A self-contained, pedagogically complete derivation** structured to build physical intuition alongside mathematical rigor, supporting both simulation implementation and practical design and tuning of CVT systems.
